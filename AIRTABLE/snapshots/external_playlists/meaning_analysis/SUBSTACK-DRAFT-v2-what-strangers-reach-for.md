# What Strangers Reach For (In A World Gone Bananas)

*3,449 quiet signals, 1,100 strangers, one wannabe humanist philosopher with a banana — and an invitation at the end.*

---

There's a thing SoundCloud never tells you: when a stranger takes one of your songs and files it into their own playlist, no notification goes out. No ping, no confetti, no dopamine hit for anyone. It just… happens, silently, in someone else's garden. Which makes it, I think, the most honest signal left on the musical internet. Nobody playlist-adds for clout. It's the difference between applause and humming a song to yourself three days later.

So I went looking. I scraped every external playlist I could find containing my music and came back with 3,449 of these quiet little acts — my songs sitting in other people's playlists, placed there by about 1,100 strangers I've never met.

And then I asked the data a question that had nothing to do with marketing:

**In a world gone bananas, what do people actually reach for?**

## First, the confession

I should introduce myself, since this is the part where you decide whether to trust me. I'm Banana. I run [bananasutra.com](https://bananasutra.com), where I write quirky philosophical lyrics — a few hundred songs organized around seven sutras, seven lenses for looking at life: know, grow, flow, glow, show, blow, bow. (Plus an eighth, quackier one, for when the news demands a duck.) I was corrupted young, in Paris, by the usual suspects — Camus, Russell, a well-placed *pourquoi?* — and never recovered. The music is made with AI tools. The words, the questions, the doubts: handmade, every one.

I know, I know. "AI music is soulless garbage." I've heard it, and a lot of it honestly is — the way a lot of everything is. But here's what this dataset let me test: if the songs were empty calories, the strangers filing them into playlists at 2am would be picking at random. Noise selects noise evenly.

They did not pick evenly. Not even close.

## What they reached for

My library is a deliberately diverse buffet — satire next to lullabies, protest next to prayer, light next to shadow. So if listeners grabbed whatever was nearest, every theme would get picked in proportion to how much of it exists on the shelf. That proportion is the baseline. The deviations are the message.

Here's the message.

**They reached for truth — at one and a half times the rate I offer it.** The most-curated song in the entire catalog, by a comical margin, is called "Tell The Truth." Not the catchiest. Not the one I'd have bet on. Just an old promise set to a slow beat: *"Speak the truth, no matter cost / In that truth, you'll never be lost."* The songbook that over-performed more than any other is called *Ask: (Naked) TRUTH* — picked at double its share of the shelf.

**They reached for ego-deflation — at double the baseline.** "Ego Ain't Your Amigo" and "The Great Naked King" sit at #2 and #3. Songs about seeing the ego, deflating it, laughing at it *"wearing nothing but bling-bling — oh, if only I was joking."* No topic in the data over-indexes harder. The emperor's tailor is doing record business, and people are hungry for the kid in the crowd.

**They reached for the fool — but not for foolery.** This one stopped me cold. Songs carrying the intention *be the fool* over-index at 2x. Songs whose *topic* is foolery — clowning for clowning's sake — under-index at half. Same jester, different job. People skip the clown who distracts them and reach for the fool who's the only one in court allowed to say the king is naked. As the Naked King himself concludes: *"Let's laugh out loud and break the ring, and be the fool worth being."* The fool as philosopher, pas le guignol.

**They reached for mortality.** My little sutra about death — bowing to it, befriending it, asking where pigeons go to die — over-indexes at 1.3. In an attention economy built on pretending we'll live forever, strangers are quietly playlisting memento mori.

**And here's what they did NOT reach for:** politics. My political satire under-performs at 0.6 — and, the part that keeps me thinking, songs tagged *be woke* sink to 0.6 too, while *be curious*, *be wise*, and *see your ego* all rise. I don't read that as apathy. I read it as exhaustion with instruction. Look at the syntax of what wins: "So, What Is Racism?" is built entirely of questions. "Everybody Knows" asks *"Can we forgive the drama, give each other grace?"* eleven different ways. People are not reaching for songs that tell them what to think. They're reaching for songs that ask them what they think. Every over-performing thread in this data interrogates; every under-performing one asserts.

One more, my favorite: **they took the shadow with the light.** My catalog is 56% light, 44% shadow, and the playlist adds came back 57/43. No toxic-positivity filter, no doom bias. Strangers accepted the dark exactly at the rate it was offered — as long as it came holding the light's hand. Which is, come to think of it, the deal life offers all of us.

(For the curious: they like it served slow, dubby, bluesy, lo-fi — in moods my tagging system calls KINDLY and CHEEKY, which over-index together. Warmth with a wink. The tempo of a walk, not a chase.)

## The part I actually wanted to tell you

Now, I could stop here and pretend this was a data story. It isn't. The data is the excuse. Here's the real one.

Every one of those songs began the same way: something in the world bothered me, or broke my heart, or made me laugh at 2am — and instead of doomscrolling past it, I had to sit with it long enough to make it rhyme. That's the whole practice. That's the secret nobody tells you about songwriting: **the song is the byproduct. The introspection is the product.**

Take "We're Tiny Specks, Right?" — a song strangers kept reaching for. On paper it's four little loops. In practice, it's me arguing myself out of nihilism in real time: *We're tiny specks… right? … And it doesn't care… right? … So we can care… right?* — until the questions dissolve and all that's left is a mantra: *Care… that's all.* I didn't write that song because I had the answer. Writing it is how I got there. Some people meditate. Some pray. I make songs — same thing, different noise. Each song's a little atheist prayer.

And this is where the seven sutras earn their keep. They're not merch (I checked; nobody's buying). They're a compass — a deliberately silly-sounding one, because I distrust solemn compasses. Before I write, I have to choose a lens: am I asking (KNOW)? Growing? Flowing? Marveling (GLOW)? Performing (SHOW)? Calling out the rot (BLOW)? Bowing to what I can't control? **Choosing your lens before you look — that's half of self-awareness right there.** The other half is noticing which lens you reach for when nobody's watching. My data says I reach for BLOW more than I'd like to admit. My strangers, bless them, keep gently handing me back KNOW.

Call it a meaningful compass. Meaningfool. Both spellings check out — rational hope with a red nose.

## So here's my actual pitch — and it's not my music

Music therapy is criminally underrated, and you don't need a therapist's referral, a record deal, or even a good voice to practice its DIY cousin. You need something that bothers you and twenty honest minutes.

Write a song. Seriously — you. Not for streams. Not for anyone. Pick the thing you can't stop turning over, pick a lens, and try to make it rhyme. Rhyme is a sneaky philosopher: it forces compression, and compression forces you to decide what you actually mean. You cannot bullshit a chorus you have to repeat four times. By verse two, you'll know things about your own position that three weeks of arguing on the internet would never have taught you.

And if the "but I can't play an instrument" wall is what stopped you until now — that wall is gone. This is the one thing the AI-music critics keep missing while they're busy being right about the slop. The tools took down the wall between people and songwriting, the same way word processors didn't kill writing and cameras didn't kill painting. What you do past the wall is still entirely, stubbornly yours. AI as Alternative Intelligence, not Artificial Indifference. Augmented Imagination, not Assisted Incompetence. Authentic imagination beats artificial indoctrination — every time. The tool doesn't decide what it's for. We do.

Honest footnote, because truth apparently is my brand: this is one artist's library on one platform; 1,100 strangers is a village, not humanity; exposure and preference feed each other in ways no spreadsheet fully untangles; and the person who labeled the themes wrote the songs. A lantern, not a telescope. But a lantern in a dark room is not nothing — and what it lit up was a village of strangers quietly reaching for truth, humility, mortality, and better questions, while walking right past the punditry.

In a world gone bananas, that's what the quiet signals say people long for. Not more noise about the madness. A way to stand in it honestly, lightly, together — preferably in three minutes, with a bassline.

Frank Zappa said music is the only religion that delivers the goods. Based on the data, I can now report what the congregation ordered: truth, served naked, with a side of humble pie. And the shadow stays in the bag — they asked for it that way.

Your pew is the second verse. Come sing.

— Banana
